Three-Statement Reps

Nobody gets fluent at this by watching. You get fluent by rebuilding the same model from a blank sheet until the sequence runs without you thinking about it — and by keeping a log of every place it broke.

■ blue = hardcoded input ■ black = formula on this sheet ■ green = link to another sheet
Where the clock should land

The benchmarks worth aiming at

These are the timings the training shops publish for a simple company built from a blank sheet. Treat them as a ladder, not a verdict — the gap between the first number and the last is almost entirely linking and debugging speed, not typing speed.

8–12h First build Your first model of a simple company. Weak Excel skills can stretch this to a full day or more.
2–3h Competent The level that reads as "can do the job." This is where repetition starts paying compounding returns.
≤90m Fluent Blank sheet to balanced model. This is the muscle-memory threshold and it is reachable with reps.

The uncomfortable part: the slow step is never the formulas. It is deciding how a line item should be driven, and finding the one link you forgot when the balance sheet is off by 4. Design your practice around those two things or you will get faster at typing and no faster at modeling.

The method

Reps become skill only when you log the break

Blocked repetition automates a sequence. Interleaved repetition — a different company, a different quirk, every time — is what makes the skill generalize instead of becoming one memorized spreadsheet. The program below does the first for two weeks, then switches to the second.

The device that turns reps into deliberate practice is a two-line entry after every single build: what broke, and how long it cost you. After ten builds you will have a ranked list of your own failure modes, and it will not be the list you expected. Drill the top item directly instead of building another whole model around it.

Rep 07 Kroger, 2h14m — balance off by 31 from FY3 break: forgot deferred rent in the CFS working-capital block cost: 26 min → drill: CFS-completeness sweep, 3× tomorrow
The program

Eight weeks, roughly twenty-five builds

Week 0 0 builds · ~4 hrs
Load the accounting, not the Excel
  • Write the three links from memory, on paper, before you open Excel: net income into retained earnings; closing cash from the CFS into the balance sheet cash line; every non-cash balance sheet change into the CFS.
  • Walk the classic interview drill out loud — depreciation rises by 10, what happens across all three statements — until it is boring.
  • Adopt the colour convention now, on day one: blue for hardcoded inputs, black for formulas, green for cross-sheet links. It is how you will find your own errors later.
Weeks 1–2 8 builds · same company
Blocked reps — automate the sequence
  • Pick one simple business and stay on it. A grocer, a restaurant chain, a single-product manufacturer — anything without securitisation, big minority interests, or heavy IFRS lease adjustments.
  • Build it once alongside a tutorial. Then close the tutorial and rebuild the same company from an empty workbook, every day, without looking.
  • Do not chase accuracy of the forecast. Chase completing the loop. A crude model that balances beats a sophisticated one that doesn't.
  • Target by the end of week 2: under three hours, unassisted, balanced on the first pass.
Weeks 3–5 9 builds · new company each
Interleave — make it generalize
  • New company every rep, rotating the business model deliberately: retail → subscription software → industrial manufacturer → services. Each one forces different working-capital behaviour, which is the whole point.
  • Add the parts you skipped: a real debt schedule, a revolver sized with MIN/MAX, interest on average balances, and the circular reference that comes with it.
  • Pull the raw numbers yourself from the 10-K on SEC EDGAR rather than a prepared template. Reading a messy filing into a clean model is half the actual job.
  • Target: 2 to 2.5 hours including the debt schedule.
Weeks 6–8 8 builds · against a clock
Compress — build under pressure
  • Run 90-minute cuts, then 60-minute cuts. When the clock beats you, stop anyway and write down exactly which step you were on.
  • Practise triage explicitly: when you can't finish, simplify so the model still balances and still answers the main question. Finishing an ugly model scores; an elegant half-model doesn't.
  • Sabotage drill — the highest-yield exercise in the program. Take a finished model, have someone (or a script) break one link at random, and time yourself finding it. Ten minutes a day here is worth an hour of building.
  • Target: 90 minutes, balanced, with a working debt schedule.
Ongoing 1 build / week
Maintain
  • One full build a week keeps it. One 60-minute timed cut a month keeps the speed.
  • Skill decays on the debugging side first, not the building side — so keep the sabotage drill in rotation even when you stop building weekly.
The sequence to automate

Nine steps, always in this order

Order matters more than people expect. Building the balance sheet before the cash flow statement, and leaving cash and the revolver until last, is what lets you defer the circular reference to a single controlled moment instead of fighting it the whole way through.

  1. Input the historicals
    Three years, typed in blue, straight from the filing. No formulas, no cleverness. Get the historical balance sheet to balance before you project anything.
  2. Calculate historical ratios and drivers
    Growth rates, margins, days receivable / inventory / payable, capex as a percentage of revenue. This is the evidence your assumptions will rest on.
  3. Set the forecast assumptions
    One clearly marked block, all blue, all in one place. Every projected line traces back here.
  4. Forecast the income statement — but stop before interest
    Down to EBIT. Interest depends on debt balances you haven't built yet, so leaving it out now is deliberate, not an oversight.
  5. Forecast the balance sheet — but leave cash and the revolver empty
    Working capital off the days ratios, PP&E off capex and depreciation, retained earnings off net income and dividends.
  6. Build the cash flow statement
    Every non-cash balance sheet line must appear here. This step is where the model is actually made or broken.
  7. Close the cash loop with MIN / MAX
    Closing cash feeds the balance sheet; the revolver draws when cash falls below the minimum and repays when it doesn't. This is the step that makes the model self-correcting.
  8. Build the interest calculations
    On average balances, against the debt schedule you just completed.
  9. Link interest back into the income statement
    This creates the circular reference on purpose. Enable iterative calculation, and keep a circuit-breaker switch that zeroes interest so you can kill a #REF! cascade without rebuilding.
Revolver draw = MAX(0, min cash − cash before revolver) Revolver repay = −MIN(revolver balance, MAX(0, cash before revolver − min cash)) Interest expense = rate × AVERAGE(opening debt, closing debt) × circuit breaker
When it doesn't balance

Check these three before anything else

The published guidance is consistent on this: in roughly 80–90% of cases the culprit is one of three broken links. Work them in order and stop guessing.

Culprit 01

Net income isn't reaching retained earnings

Rebuild net income independently — revenue less COGS, opex, D&A, interest, tax — and compare. If it doesn't match, your error is on the income statement, not the balance sheet.

Culprit 02

Closing cash isn't feeding the balance sheet

Check the opening cash cell too. When you copy a base case to build a downside, opening cash very often stays hardcoded to the base-case number instead of linking to the prior period.

Culprit 03

A balance sheet line is missing from the CFS

Highlight every balance sheet line as you reference it in the cash flow statement. Any non-zero line left unhighlighted is, by definition, your error.

Sign convention — the rule that prevents half of all imbalances Assets: change = prior period − current period Liabilities & equity: change = current period − prior period

Every break falls into one of three families: signage (right line, wrong direction), omission (line never made it to the CFS), or classification (right number, wrong section). Naming the family in your rep log is more useful than naming the cell.

The layer underneath

Get your hands off the mouse

This is the part that is genuinely muscle memory rather than knowledge, and it is worth training separately from model building. Physically move the mouse out of reach for a fixed block each day. The point isn't raw speed — it's that navigation stops consuming attention you need for the accounting.

KeyWhat it buys you
Ctrl + arrowJump to the edge of a data block. This is how you move around a model at all without a mouse.
Ctrl + Shift + arrowSelect to the edge — the basis of every fast copy across a forecast row.
F2Edit in cell and see the precedents highlighted in place. Your fastest first look at a suspect formula.
Ctrl + [Jump to the precedents of a formula. The single most useful debugging key there is.
F5 → SpecialSelect all constants in a range — instantly exposes hardcodes hiding inside a formula row.
Alt + =AutoSum. Subtotal rows without reaching for anything.
Alt + H + 0Ribbon key sequences — learn the four or five you use constantly rather than all of them.

One claim to ignore: several blogs advertise a specific percentage speed gain from going mouse-free within a fixed number of days. That figure is marketing, with no study behind it. The habit is well worth building; the number is invented.
