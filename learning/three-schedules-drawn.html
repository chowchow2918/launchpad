The two that keep slipping

Three Schedules, Drawn

The PP&E chain that vanished from your last rep, and the tax pair you keep filing as a flow when half of it is a balance. Each one drawn as the mechanism, so the reason it exists is visible rather than memorised.

Figure 1

PP&E: one balance, two flows, three destinations

This is the textbook case for why schedules exist at all. The balance rolls forward, but the two things that move it go to different statements — and depreciation goes to two places at once. Bury that inside a single balance sheet formula and there is nowhere for the income statement to read the depreciation from.

Why it vanished as a set. Your last rep lost the asset schedule and the depreciation driver together, while keeping depreciation on the income statement. That is one omission, not two — the chain that produces the number went, and only the place it lands survived.

Figure 2

Tax expense is a row. Income taxes payable is a schedule.

Same word, two entirely different objects. Tax expense is a flow you calculate. Income taxes payable is a balance that accumulates. Applying your own test from earlier: one has no memory of last period and one destination, the other rolls forward and has to appear in current liabilities.

What a fuller tax schedule adds, and why you can skip it. A more elaborate one splits current from deferred tax, tracks NOL carryforwards, and reconciles the statutory rate to the effective rate. None of that belongs in a simple 3-statement model — it changes the answer only when there are real NOLs or big book-tax differences. Effective rate × EBT is the right level for every rep between now and week five.

Figure 3

The days-driven version you actually set up

Your assumptions block has Income Tax Payable Days, which means you are modelling it the same way you model receivables and payables — not with the explicit paid-in-cash line from figure 2. That is the right call for a simple model, and it is worth seeing what it computes.

For the next rep

The three lines to check before you call it done

All three of these are the same failure in different clothing — the flow gets written down and the balance doesn't. Put them at the front of your closing sweep:

1. Does the asset schedule exist, and does depreciation have a driver? 2. Is income taxes payable in current liabilities on the balance sheet? 3. For every non-cash balance sheet line, is its change on the cash flow statement? …and for every change on the cash flow statement, is there a balance it came from?

Point three runs in both directions on purpose. You have been reliable in one direction and not the other, and reading it backwards is what would have caught income taxes payable every time.
